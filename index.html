How this map is made

  • Warming is the change in near-surface air temperature vs the 1850-1900 average of the same grid cell.
  • Averaging in time: monthly values are averaged to years, then smoothed with a 10 year centered average so one hot year cannot count as a crossing. The last few years use shorter averaging windows.
  • Crossing year: each cell is colored by the first year that smoothed warming reaches the threshold. Purple means it was already there in 2000, the first year shown. Gray means it never gets there by 2100.
  • 1° grid: the model's native grid is interpolated onto a 1° by 1° grid (180 × 360 cells, each about 100 km wide), close to the model's own resolution, so detail finer than that is not available.
  • Area weighting: the "% of the globe's area" figures weight each cell by cos(latitude), because cells near the poles cover less area than cells near the equator.
  • One model, one run: all data comes from a single climate model (GFDL-ESM4, ensemble member r1i1p1f1) under three emissions scenarios. With no spread between models, each map is one possible path, not a forecast, and other models would give different crossing years.

Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan (the map loops sideways), double-click to zoom in.

Project Write-Up

1. Motivation

Climate change is often discussed through global averages, such as the goal of limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C. These averages hide a lot. Land generally warms faster than ocean, and the Arctic warms much faster than most of the tropics, so a "global" threshold is reached at different times in different places.

We wanted to explore this with a specific question: when will different parts of the world first reach a given level of warming, and how does that change depending on future emissions?

Instead of plotting temperature over time for one location, our map shows the year each location first reaches a selected warming threshold. This lets a viewer see where warming arrives first, how large the gaps in timing are between regions, and how much those patterns change between emissions scenarios. Changing the threshold and the scenario lets the viewer see that warming is not experienced equally across the globe and that the outcome depends partly on future emissions.

2. Data and Transformations

2.1 Source data

We used CMIP6 climate model output from Google Cloud's public Zarr collection, read with xarray and gcsfs from the catalog CSV. We used monthly near-surface air temperature (tas, table Amon) from the GFDL-ESM4 model, ensemble member r1i1p1f1, because it has the historical run and all three scenarios we wanted (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) for the same member. The historical run covers 1850-2014 and the scenario runs cover 2015-2100. The model stores longitude from 0° to 360°, so we shifted it to -180° to 180° and re-sorted it to match a normal world map.

2.2 Warming relative to a baseline

For every grid cell we averaged the monthly temperatures from 1850-1900 to get that cell's own pre-industrial baseline, and subtracted it from later temperatures. Temperatures are stored in kelvin, but a change in kelvin equals a change in °C, so no conversion was needed. Using each cell's own baseline means we compare how much places have warmed, not how hot they normally are.

2.3 Yearly values and smoothing

We averaged the monthly values into one value per year, then applied a 10-year centered moving average so one unusually hot year cannot count as a threshold crossing. To make the smoothing work at the start of the map, we joined 1985-2014 from the historical run to the 2015-2100 scenario run, smoothed the combined series, and kept 2000-2100. The last few years have no later years to average, so they use a shorter window (at least 5 years).

2.4 Regridding to 1°

We linearly interpolated the data onto a regular 1° by 1° grid of cell centers (latitude -89.5 to 89.5, longitude -179.5 to 179.5), giving 180 × 360 = 64,800 cells. This is close to the model's own resolution, so it does not add detail the model does not have. The interpolation was done before the baseline subtraction and smoothing.

2.5 Finding the crossing year

For each cell, threshold and scenario, the crossing year is the first year from 2000 to 2100 in which the smoothed warming is at or above the threshold. If that never happens the cell is marked "never" (gray). A cell that is already at or above the threshold in 2000 gets the year 2000, which we color purple. We did this for five thresholds (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 and 3°C) and three scenarios, so 15 maps. This is the first year the smoothed warming reaches the threshold, not the year it stays above it permanently.

2.6 Preparing the data for the web

Because the page cannot use a server, we computed all 15 maps ahead of time in the notebook and wrote them to a single static file, data/crossing_data.js. Each map is a flat list of 64,800 whole numbers (rows of latitude, columns of longitude, with null for "never"), and the page loads the file with an ordinary script tag. In the browser, moving the slider or changing the scenario only recolors the map, and nothing about the climate is recomputed.

2.7 Area-weighted statistics

The percentages shown above the map weight each cell by the cosine of its latitude. A 1° cell near a pole covers much less area than one at the equator, so without this the polar regions would count for too much.

3. Visual Design and Rationale

3.1 Map Design

We chose a world map because the question is about where and when warming happens, and a map makes patterns like land versus ocean or high versus low latitude easy to see. Each grid cell is one colored square. We used an equirectangular layout because it matches our regular latitude-longitude grid, so every cell stays the same shape on screen and the data maps directly onto the page without a projection.

The map can be zoomed with scroll or pinch, panned by dragging, and zoomed in with a double-click. It loops sideways, so a viewer can drag across the International Date Line without hitting an edge. The longitude axis relabels as the viewer pans and zooms.

3.2 Color Encoding

The crossing year is ordered and continuous, so we used a sequential YlOrRd palette (the same one we used in the notebook plot): early years are dark red and later years are pale yellow, so the places that warm first stand out. The color bar runs from 2001 to 2100.

Two colors sit outside that scale so they are not mistaken for ordinary years:

  • Purple: the place was already at or above the threshold in 2000, the first year shown, so we cannot tell when it actually crossed.
  • Gray: the place never reaches the threshold by 2100.

The legend under the map shows both swatches next to the color bar, and its label updates with the selected threshold.

3.3 Interaction and Additional Information

There are three controls: a threshold slider (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 or 3°C), an emissions scenario dropdown, and a reset-view button. The threshold slider lets the viewer ask how soon different levels of warming arrive. The scenario dropdown holds the threshold fixed while the viewer compares futures, which shows which regions cross earlier under high emissions and which avoid the threshold altogether.

Hovering over the map shows a tooltip with the latitude, longitude and crossing year of that cell, so the map is not covered in labels. The subtitle above the map gives the area-weighted percentage of the globe that is already there in 2000 and the percentage that gets there by 2100, which gives a global summary that the colors alone do not.

3.4 Limitations and Uncertainty

The map uses one climate model and one ensemble member, so it shows one possible path and not a forecast. Other models or runs would give different crossing years. The 1° grid also cannot show small-scale differences, so the map is meant to show broad patterns and not local predictions. We put a "How this map is made" section under the map so these limits are visible next to the graphic.

4. Development Process

4.1 Exploration and Planning

We started in a Jupyter notebook by exploring the CMIP6 catalog and the GFDL-ESM4 data. Our early plots were: global warming over time under each scenario, maps of warming for each decade, low versus high emissions side by side, warming by latitude, San Diego versus the global average, and the change in rainfall under high emissions. The decade maps showed how much warming there is, but we wanted the viewer to see when each place reaches a level, so we switched to mapping the first year a threshold is crossed, which gave us one clear question to build around.

4.2 Data Processing

All the processing described in section 2 was done in the notebook: loading and aligning the data, baselines, yearly averages, smoothing, regridding, crossing years, and exporting the 15 maps to data/crossing_data.js.

4.3 Building the Visualization

We built the page with HTML, CSS and D3.js only (D3 v7 from a CDN, no other plotting library), split into index.html, style.css, vis.js and the data file. D3 provides the scales, axes, color bar, zoom behavior, tooltip and controls. Our first version drew each of the 64,800 cells as its own SVG rectangle with a color transition, which was slow when the threshold or scenario changed. We changed it to color the cells in a small offscreen canvas and show that as one SVG image, which keeps recoloring fast, and the zoom, tooltip and axes are still done with D3.

4.4 Iteration and Refinement

As we built it we fixed several problems that only showed up in the browser:

  • Our exported data was still on a coarser 5° grid while the latitude and longitude lists said 1°, so only a strip along the bottom of the map was filled in. We re-exported the data at 1° and made the page work out the grid size from the data so the two cannot disagree.
  • A gray stripe appeared at the International Date Line. The model's longitudes stop slightly short of ±180°, so the two edge columns of our 1° grid were empty after interpolation. We fixed it by extrapolating the last fraction of a degree when regridding.
  • When panning past the edge, a thin line appeared where the two copies of the world met. We fixed it by drawing both copies in one image so there is no seam.

We also added the purple and gray categories, the legend, the subtitle statistics, the sideways looping, the reset button and the methodology section.

4.5 Future Improvements

The biggest improvement would be adding more climate models and ensemble members so we could show how much the crossing years vary instead of relying on one run. We would also like to let a viewer click a place to see its full temperature history, so they can see how it approaches the threshold and not only the final crossing year.

5. Reflection

The way the data is presented changes the questions people ask. Showing temperature over time answers "how much", while mapping the first year a threshold is reached answers "when and where", and the map makes the geographic differences much easier to see.

We also learned that a lot of work happens outside the chart. Decisions about baselines, smoothing, grid size, color, and how to state limitations all change how a viewer reads the result, and bugs in the data (like the stale grid and the date line stripe) only appeared when we looked at the actual map.

We hope the map shows that climate change is not a single global number, and that where and when it arrives depends on both location and the path emissions take.
